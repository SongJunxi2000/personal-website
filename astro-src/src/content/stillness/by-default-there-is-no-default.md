> 代际传递，指创伤会通过抚养方式传递到下一辈，而重大创伤可能需要三代人，甚至五代人来消化和复原。

最近在思考一个问题：如果不出意外，我这辈子应该都会有食物、住所，以及感到安全和温暖，但是为什么我在做选择的时候，依然非常受限于这件事能否给我带来额外的资源，而不是考虑我即将要做的这件事，我会不会喜欢，以及这个世界会不会喜欢。

与之相对，让我想起了 misof。二十四年的时候我邀请他来中国，作为曾经的竞赛选手，也是 IOI 的前主席，他可谓是"躺得非常平"：教教课，当当 advisor，和女朋友攀攀岩。他落地上海的时候，背着书包站在浦东机场，像一个来旅游的高中生。我那个时候第一次具象化了欧洲人的"松弛感"。我看到了一些在中国和美国都没有见过的东西，但是当时并不知道那是什么。

自从我二十三年离开 CMU，听到最多的问题就是为什么，似乎所有人都无法想象，人会主动放弃资源，尤其是这个资源如此稀有。其实我也无法想象。我想我的离开其实是一种恐惧，我害怕在资源争夺的路上无休无止地走下去，害怕永远拼杀在更好的学校、更大的 offer、更高的 title、更贵的房子的路上。

我似乎永远都在可以获取更多资源的时候，毫不犹豫地去掠夺它，似乎并没有空间和自由去思考这到底是不是我想要的。而这种对资源不经思考的获取，让我感到恐惧，以至于我采取了另外一种简单粗暴的做法：远离资源。我不理解为什么在资源出现的时候，我无法做出拒绝的选择，这个状态让我感到非常糟糕，让我感觉到失控。或许在无法做出选择的时候，站在漩涡中间也是一种选择。

七年的谈话好像都没能解释为什么我会有这种恐惧和反抗，直到现在，我才渐渐意识到，这是一种对于尚未觉察的知觉的尚未察觉的防御。

最近我开始系统地学习心理咨询的课程。对于一个在过去的七年里完全重塑了我的事件，我开始尝试系统地学习和了解它，然后学到了家庭治疗。家庭治疗的创始人鲍恩提出了一个至关重要的概念，叫做多代传递过程，是指家庭里的情绪反应和相处方式，会在抚养的过程中传递到下一代，并且一代一代地累积。课程里老师举了很多例子：六十年代举家迁移到新疆的家庭，内部形成了一种堡垒的形式，来对抗新的物理环境和政治环境所带来的潜在风险；而六十年后，尽管这种风险已经不复存在，父亲仍然要想尽一切办法阻止孩子离开新疆，离开这个堡垒，仿佛外部的风险从来没有消失。

这个概念似乎对我的困惑提供了一种可能的解释。我的外婆很喜欢在家里囤很多食物，甚至导致很多食物只有在不新鲜的时候才有机会被吃掉。我想，这种对资源匮乏的不安全感，或许就是一种创伤。在这个重大的创伤下，在任何可能的时候去争取更多的资源，被写进了大脑的通路中，然后一代一代地传递到了我这儿。

时至今日，当初造成这种行为的原因已经消失。得益于轻工业的发展和密集的人口，中国或许在一定程度上已经成为这个世界上物质资源最丰富的地方，食物和基础的生活用品从来没有如此触手可及，但是我依然在一遍一遍地重复着对资源的渴望和获取，它就像轮回。

但是我相信所有的创伤最终都可以被修复。心理咨询从弗洛伊德到卡尔·罗杰斯，走到今天，全世界的主题都是去中心化。这个让我亏了无数钱的概念，其实我从来都没有理解过。直到今天，我才意识到，或许去中心化真正的意义在于去默认值，而去默认值是所有思考的开始，就像灵活是所有复原力的开始。

只有我们真正放弃所有对于默认的默认，放弃这个在过去上千年的历史中帮助人类节约能量、节约 brain power、极大程度增加了存活概率的功能时，我们才能真正开始看见自己，开始看见生命的力量和意义。

“Between stimulus and response there is a space. In that space is our freedom.”

> Intergenerational transmission means that trauma is passed down through the ways we raise our children, and that profound trauma may take three generations, or even five, to process and recover from.

Lately I've been thinking about a question: barring anything unexpected, I should have food, shelter, and a sense of safety and warmth for the rest of my life. Why, then, are my choices still so constrained by whether something will bring me additional resources, instead of whether I will like what I'm about to do, and whether the world will like it too?

By contrast, I think of misof. In 2024, I invited him to China. A former competitive programmer and former president of the IOI, he could be said to have thoroughly embraced “lying flat”: teaching a few classes, working as an advisor, going climbing with his girlfriend. When he landed in Shanghai, he stood in Pudong Airport with a backpack, looking like a high school student on holiday. For the first time, that European sense of ease became something tangible to me. I saw something I had never seen in either China or the United States, though at the time I didn't know what it was.

Since I left CMU in 2023, the question I've heard most often is why. It seems no one can imagine someone willingly giving up resources, especially resources so rare. In truth, I can't quite imagine it either. I think my leaving was really a kind of fear: I was afraid of continuing endlessly down the path of competing for resources, afraid of spending my life fighting for a better school, a bigger offer, a higher title, a more expensive house.

Whenever more resources are within reach, I seem to seize them without hesitation, with no room or freedom to ask whether they are what I actually want. This thoughtless acquisition frightens me so much that I adopted another blunt approach: staying away from resources. I don't understand why, when resources appear, I cannot choose to refuse them. It feels terrible. It makes me feel out of control. Perhaps, when I cannot make a choice, standing in the middle of the vortex is also a choice.

Seven years of counseling seemed unable to explain this fear and resistance. Only now am I slowly beginning to realize that this is an unnoticed defense against a perception I have not yet become aware of.

Recently I began taking courses in psychological counseling. Having been completely reshaped by it over the past seven years, I wanted to study and understand it more systematically. That led me to family therapy. Bowen, a founder of family therapy, introduced a crucial concept called the multigenerational transmission process: emotional responses and ways of relating within a family are passed down through child-rearing, accumulating from one generation to the next. Our teacher gave many examples. Families who moved together to Xinjiang in the 1960s formed a kind of fortress within the family to defend against the potential risks of a new physical and political environment. Sixty years later, even though those risks no longer existed, a father would still do everything he could to stop his child from leaving Xinjiang, from leaving that fortress, as if the dangers outside had never disappeared.

This concept seems to offer one possible explanation for my confusion. My grandmother likes to stockpile food at home, to the point that much of it only gets eaten once it is no longer fresh. I wonder whether this insecurity about scarcity is itself a kind of trauma. Under the weight of that profound trauma, the impulse to secure more resources whenever possible became written into the brain's circuits, then passed down, generation after generation, until it reached me.

Today, the original cause of this behavior has disappeared. Thanks to the development of light industry and a dense population, China may, in some ways, have become the most materially abundant place in the world. Food and basic necessities have never been so readily available. Yet I keep repeating the same longing for resources and the same pursuit of them. It feels like a cycle of rebirth.

But I believe all trauma can eventually be healed. From Freud to Carl Rogers to the present day, the theme, in counseling and across the world, has been decentralization. This concept, which has cost me so much money, is something I never really understood. Only today did I realize that perhaps the true meaning of decentralization is to let go of defaults. Letting go of defaults is where all thinking begins, just as flexibility is where all resilience begins.

Only when we truly let go of every default assumption about defaults—when we relinquish this function that has helped humans conserve energy and brain power, and greatly increased our chances of survival over thousands of years—can we begin to see ourselves, and begin to see the strength and meaning of life.

“Between stimulus and response there is a space. In that space is our freedom.”
